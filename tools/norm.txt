Neuropathic pain
Total points5/10

A 68-year-old man presents to your clinic with a 3-month history of severe
burning pain on the left side of his chest. He had a vesicular rash in the same area
4 months ago that has since healed. The pain is constant, rated 7/10, and is
accompanied by extreme sensitivity to touch in the affected area. He describes
even light clothing as painful. He has tried acetaminophen with minimal relief. His
medical history includes well-controlled hypertension. Physical examination
reveals an area of healed skin with some scarring in a T5 dermatome distribution
on the left.
Question: Which one of the following is the most likely diagnosis? Select 1 or
select "None" if none is indicated.

1/1
Acute herpes zoster
Postherpetic neuralgia

Intercostal neuralgia
Cardiac angina
Diabetic neuropathy
None
Feedback
Correct Answer: 2. Postherpetic neuralgia
Rationale: This patient has classic postherpetic neuralgia (PHN). The key diagnostic
features include:
History of vesicular rash (herpes zoster) 4 months ago
===PAGE 2===
Persistent pain continuing beyond healing of the rash (>3 months)
Dermatomal distribution (T5)
Burning pain quality
Allodynia (pain from non-noxious stimuli - light touch)
Location in thoracic dermatome (50% of herpes zoster cases affect thoracic
dermatomes)
Postherpetic neuralgia is defined as pain persisting after the acute herpes zoster
infection has resolved. The risk increases with age and severity of the eruption.
Why other options are incorrect:
Option 1 (Acute herpes zoster): The rash has healed and the pain has persisted for
3 months after rash resolution. This is chronic, not acute.
Option 3 (Intercostal neuralgia): While this could cause chest wall pain, there is no
history of trauma or other cause, and the clear temporal relationship with herpes
zoster makes PHN the correct diagnosis.
Option 4 (Cardiac angina): Angina is not associated with skin sensitivity, does not
have a dermatomal distribution, and typically relates to exertion rather than being
constant.
Option 5 (Diabetic neuropathy): Patient has no history of diabetes, and diabetic
neuropathy typically presents in a stocking-glove distribution, not dermatomal.

Feedback
Correct Answer: 2. Amitriptyline 10 mg at bedtime
Rationale: Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are first-line agents for chronic
peripheral neuropathic pain, including postherpetic neuralgia. The appropriate
approach is to "start low, go slow":
Initial dose: 10-25 mg at bedtime
Increase by 10-25 mg at weekly intervals
Titrate until pain relief or side effects occur
Amitriptyline 10 mg at bedtime represents the correct starting dose for this elderly
patient. Starting at a low dose minimizes side effects (dry mouth, constipation,
drowsiness, urinary retention, confusion, cardiac effects) which are particularly
important in elderly patients.
Why other options are incorrect:
Option 1 (Amitriptyline 75 mg at bedtime): This dose is too high to start with in an
elderly patient. The risk of side effects (especially confusion, falls, anticholinergic
effects) would be unacceptable. This might be a target dose after gradual titration.
Option 3 (Nortriptyline 50 mg at bedtime): While nortriptyline is an appropriate
TCA choice (may have fewer anticholinergic side effects than amitriptyline), 50 mg
is too high as a starting dose. Should start at 10-25 mg.
Option 4 (Desipramine 100 mg at bedtime): Desipramine is another appropriate
TCA, but 100 mg is far too high as a starting dose.
Option 5 (Fluoxetine 20 mg daily): Fluoxetine is an SSRI, not a TCA. SSRIs are not
effective for neuropathic pain. TCAs and SNRIs (duloxetine, venlafaxine) are the
antidepressants with evidence for neuropathic pain.

Correct Answer: 3. Carbamazepine 100 mg twice daily
Rationale: This patient has classic trigeminal neuralgia (tic douloureux). The
diagnostic features include:
Electric shock-like quality of pain
Brief duration (seconds)
Trigger zones (nasolabial region)

Carbamazepine is the first-line treatment for trigeminal neuralgia and is highly
effective for this specific condition. It is generally NOT effective for other types of
neuropathic pain unless there is a shock-like component. The appropriate starting
dose is 100 mg BID-QID, increased by 100 mg daily at weekly intervals until good
pain control or intolerable side effects.
Why other options are incorrect:
Option 1 (Amitriptyline): While TCAs are first-line for most chronic peripheral
neuropathic pain conditions (e.g., postherpetic neuralgia, diabetic neuropathy),
they are inferior to carbamazepine for trigeminal neuralgia.
Option 2 (Gabapentin): GABA derivatives are first-line for general neuropathic pain
but are not as effective as carbamazepine for trigeminal neuralgia. Gabapentin
may be tried if carbamazepine fails.
Option 4 (Morphine): Opioids are third-line agents for neuropathic pain and should
not be used as first-line therapy. Carbamazepine is highly effective for trigeminal
neuralgia.
Option 5 (Topical lidocaine): Topical agents are not appropriate for trigeminal
neuralgia, which requires systemic therapy.

total is likely too low to provide adequate pain relief.

Correct Answer: 2. Morphine immediate-release 12 mg as needed
Rationale: For breakthrough pain in patients on sustained-release opioids, the

10% of total daily dose: 120 mg × 0.10 = 12 mg
Therefore: Morphine immediate-release 12 mg PRN for breakthrough pain

(more than 2-3 per day regularly), the baseline controlled-release dose should be
increased.
