# Endocrinology — Practice Questions, Set 2

USMLE Step 2 CK level. Twenty original questions. Answers and explanations are at the bottom.

## Questions

**1.** A 34-year-old woman with untreated Graves disease is brought in 1 day after an appendectomy. Temperature is 40.1°C, pulse 152/min and irregular, and she is agitated and confused. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

## Answers and explanations

**1. D — Block hormone action and synthesis first, then give iodine.** Thyroid storm is treated with a beta blocker, a thionamide (propylthiouracil also blocks T4-to-T3 conversion), iodine given at least an hour after the thionamide so it is not used to make more hormone, and glucocorticoids. Aspirin is avoided because it frees thyroid hormone from its binding proteins; acetaminophen is used for fever.

**2. A — IV levothyroxine with hydrocortisone.** Myxedema coma is severe hypothyroidism with hypothermia, low sodium, slow heart rate, and confusion, and it is often fatal. Thyroid hormone is given IV, and stress-dose hydrocortisone is given until coexisting adrenal insufficiency is excluded, because thyroid hormone can provoke adrenal crisis.

**3. B — Propylthiouracil in the first trimester.** Methimazole can cause birth defects (such as scalp aplasia cutis) when used in early pregnancy, so propylthiouracil is used until about 16 weeks, then switched to methimazole because of propylthiouracil's liver toxicity. Radioactive iodine is contraindicated in pregnancy.

**4. D — Rapid IV normal saline.** Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state causes severe dehydration, often 8–10 L. Isotonic fluid is the first and most important step; insulin is started after fluids are running and potassium is known, and fluid alone lowers glucose substantially.

**5. C — Surreptitious insulin.** Injected insulin raises insulin but suppresses the pancreas, so C-peptide (released with natural insulin) is low. Insulinoma and sulfonylureas raise both insulin and C-peptide. Healthcare workers have access to insulin.

**6. A — Sulfonylurea screen.** High insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin with hypoglycemia means the pancreas is overproducing insulin, either from an insulinoma or from a sulfonylurea or meglitinide. Only a drug screen tells them apart, and it must be negative before imaging for insulinoma.

**7. D — Plasma free metanephrines.** Medullary thyroid cancer with a family history of adrenal tumors suggests MEN 2 (RET mutation). A pheochromocytoma must be ruled out and removed first, because operating on the thyroid with an untreated pheochromocytoma can cause a hypertensive crisis.

**8. C — Parathyroidectomy.** Surgery is advised in asymptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism when calcium is more than 1 mg/dL above the upper limit, T-score is −2.5 or lower, eGFR is below 60, there are kidney stones or high urine calcium, or age is under 50. Her calcium is 1.4 mg/dL above the limit.

**9. A — IV calcium gluconate.** Symptomatic hypocalcemia after thyroidectomy (from parathyroid injury or removal) with a positive Chvostek sign is treated with IV calcium. Oral calcium and calcitriol are then started, and magnesium is checked and corrected.

**10. B — Central diabetes insipidus.** Dilute urine that does not concentrate with dehydration but does concentrate after desmopressin means the kidney works but ADH is missing. Head trauma, pituitary surgery, and tumors are common causes. In nephrogenic DI, desmopressin produces little change.

**11. A — Amiloride.** Lithium enters collecting-duct cells through the sodium channel and causes nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Amiloride blocks that channel and reduces urine output; thiazides also help. Desmopressin does not work because the kidney cannot respond to ADH.

**12. C — Insulin-like growth factor 1.** IGF-1 reflects average growth hormone over time and is the best screening test for acromegaly. A random GH level is unreliable because GH is released in pulses. A high IGF-1 is confirmed by failure of GH to suppress after an oral glucose load, then pituitary MRI.

**13. A — Sheehan syndrome.** The pituitary enlarges in pregnancy and is vulnerable to infarction with severe blood loss. Failure to lactate (no prolactin) is often the first sign, followed by amenorrhea, hypothyroidism, and adrenal insufficiency. Cortisol replacement is started before thyroid hormone.

**14. B — Hormone testing.** Every adrenal incidentaloma is checked for cortisol excess (1-mg dexamethasone suppression) and pheochromocytoma (metanephrines), and for aldosterone excess if there is hypertension or low potassium. A small, uniform, low-density mass is benign-looking, so no repeat imaging is usually needed if hormone tests are normal.

**15. C — High 17-hydroxyprogesterone.** 21-hydroxylase deficiency is the most common form of congenital adrenal hyperplasia. Cortisol and aldosterone cannot be made, so ACTH rises, precursors are diverted to androgens (virilizing girls), and salt wasting causes low sodium and high potassium.

**16. A — Start levothyroxine.** In pregnancy, a TSH above the trimester-specific range with positive TPO antibodies is treated, because untreated hypothyroidism is linked to miscarriage and impaired fetal brain development. Thyroid hormone needs rise in pregnancy.

**17. D — Start low and go slow.** In older adults and those with coronary disease, a full dose can increase heart oxygen demand and trigger angina or arrhythmia. Levothyroxine is started at about 12.5–25 μg daily and increased every 4–6 weeks based on TSH.

**18. A — 3-hour glucose tolerance test.** In the two-step approach, an abnormal 1-hour screen (commonly 130–140 mg/dL or higher) is followed by a diagnostic 3-hour 100-g test. Gestational diabetes is diagnosed if two or more values are high. Treatment starts only after diagnosis.

**19. C — Confirm with a repeat test.** In someone without clear symptoms, diabetes is diagnosed by two abnormal results, either the same test repeated or two different tests. A single HbA1c of 6.5% or higher is not enough unless glucose is clearly high (200 mg/dL or more) with typical symptoms.

**20. A — Stress-dose steroids.** Anyone taking 20 mg or more of prednisone for more than 3 weeks should be assumed to have adrenal suppression. Major surgery calls for stress-dose hydrocortisone around the operation, then a return to the usual dose. Stopping steroids beforehand risks adrenal crisis.
