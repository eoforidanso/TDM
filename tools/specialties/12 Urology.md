# Urology — Practice Questions, Set 2

USMLE Step 2 CK level. Twenty original questions. Answers and explanations are at the bottom.

## Questions

## Answers and explanations

**1. C — CT scan of the abdomen.** Most varicoceles are left-sided because the left testicular vein drains at a right angle into the left renal vein. A new right-sided or non-reducing varicocele raises concern for a retroperitoneal or renal mass compressing the vein.

**2. D — Scrotal ultrasound.** A hydrocele transilluminates, but in an adult, especially if the testis cannot be felt, ultrasound is needed to exclude an underlying tumor, infection, or torsion that can cause a reactive hydrocele.

**3. B — Active surveillance.** Low-risk prostate cancer (grade group 1, low PSA, small volume) rarely progresses. Active surveillance with PSA, examination, MRI, and repeat biopsies avoids the urinary and sexual side effects of surgery or radiation while keeping cure possible.

**4. C — Renal cell carcinoma.** The classic triad is flank pain, blood in the urine, and a flank mass, though few patients have all three. The tumor can make erythropoietin (high hemoglobin) and invade the left renal vein, causing a left varicocele. Smoking is the main risk factor.

**5. A — Potassium citrate.** Uric acid stones are invisible on plain X-ray and form in acidic urine. Raising urine pH to 6–6.5 with potassium citrate dissolves and prevents them; allopurinol is added if uric acid excretion is high.

**6. B — Normal calcium, less salt and animal protein.** Dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut, so low-calcium diets increase stone risk. Sodium and animal protein increase urine calcium. High fluid intake is the most important single measure; a thiazide helps with high urine calcium.

**7. D — Percutaneous nephrolithotomy.** Struvite (staghorn) stones form in alkaline urine from urease-producing bacteria such as Proteus. The stone harbors bacteria, so it must be removed completely, and percutaneous surgery is best for large stones.

**8. E — Tadalafil.** Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors combined with nitrates cause severe, potentially fatal low blood pressure. Other ED treatments, such as a vacuum device or alprostadil injection, can be used.

**9. E — Bladder training.** Behavioral treatment — timed voiding, urge suppression, and reducing caffeine and evening fluids — is first-line for urge incontinence. Anticholinergics are avoided where possible in older adults because of confusion, and mirabegron is an alternative.

**10. E — Pelvic floor muscle exercises.** Stress incontinence comes from weak pelvic floor support of the urethra, often after childbirth. Kegel exercises are first-line; a mid-urethral sling is for those who do not improve. Anticholinergics treat urge, not stress, incontinence.

**11. D — Intermittent self-catheterization.** Diabetic neuropathy can leave the bladder unable to contract, so it overfills and overflows. Regular catheterization empties it and protects the kidneys. Anticholinergics would worsen retention.

**12. E — Vigorous prostatic massage.** In acute bacterial prostatitis, massage is painful and can spread bacteria into the blood. Diagnosis rests on symptoms, a tender prostate, and urine culture; treatment is antibiotics such as a fluoroquinolone or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for several weeks.

**13. E — Pure seminoma.** Pure seminomas never make alpha-fetoprotein. A high AFP means non-seminomatous elements, usually yolk sac tumor, are present, and the tumor is treated as non-seminomatous, which changes therapy.

**14. D — Orchiopexy now.** Testes not descended by about 6 months rarely descend later. Surgery is recommended between 6 and 18 months to protect fertility and allow cancer surveillance. Hormone therapy is not recommended, and ultrasound does not change management.

**15. B — Do not circumcise.** The foreskin is used to repair hypospadias, so circumcision is postponed. Repair is usually done between 6 and 18 months. Karyotyping is reserved for those with an undescended testis or ambiguous genitalia.

**16. D — Manual reduction.** Paraphimosis is an emergency: the trapped foreskin acts as a tourniquet and can cut off blood flow to the glans. Swelling is reduced with compression or ice, then the foreskin is pushed back; if that fails, a dorsal slit is done.

**17. D — Retrograde CT cystography.** Gross hematuria with a pelvic fracture suggests bladder rupture. The bladder must be actively filled with contrast to show a leak; a standard CT without filling can miss it. Extraperitoneal ruptures are often managed with a catheter, intraperitoneal ones with surgery.

**18. D — Emergency debridement and antibiotics.** Fournier gangrene is necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum, most common in men with diabetes. Pain out of proportion and crepitus are warning signs. It is fatal without prompt surgical removal of dead tissue; imaging must not delay surgery.

**19. C — Voiding cystourethrogram.** Posterior urethral valves are the most common cause of bladder outlet obstruction in newborn boys. The VCUG shows a dilated posterior urethra during voiding. Valves are removed endoscopically, with long-term kidney follow-up.

**20. B — Shared decision-making.** For men aged 55–69, PSA screening offers a small reduction in prostate cancer deaths with real risks of false positives, biopsies, and overtreatment. The decision should follow a discussion of the patient's values. Screening is not recommended after 70.
