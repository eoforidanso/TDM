# Dermatology — Practice Questions, Set 2

USMLE Step 2 CK level. Twenty original questions. Answers and explanations are at the bottom.

## Questions

## Answers and explanations

**1. C — Breslow thickness.** The depth of invasion, measured in millimeters from the top of the granular layer, is the strongest predictor of survival in localized melanoma and determines margins and the need for sentinel node biopsy. Ulceration and mitotic rate also matter.

**2. E — Look for internal cancer.** Sudden eruption of many seborrheic keratoses (the sign of Leser–Trélat) is a rare sign of internal cancer, most often gastric adenocarcinoma. Weight loss adds to the concern. Ordinary seborrheic keratoses are benign and need no treatment.

**3. D — Fasting glucose or HbA1c.** Acanthosis nigricans most commonly signals insulin resistance (obesity, prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome). Sudden, extensive acanthosis in a thin older adult raises concern for gastric cancer.

**4. E — Topical selenium sulfide or ketoconazole.** Tinea versicolor is caused by Malassezia yeast. The patches do not tan, so they show after sun exposure. The "spaghetti and meatballs" appearance on KOH is typical. It often recurs, so periodic preventive treatment is used.

**5. D — Permethrin for everyone in the house.** Scabies spreads through close contact. All household members are treated at the same time, even without symptoms, and clothing and bedding are washed hot. Itching can last weeks after successful treatment.

**6. C — Topical mupirocin.** Limited impetigo is treated with a topical antibiotic. Oral antibiotics are for extensive disease, outbreaks, or bullous impetigo. Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes are the usual causes.

**7. C — Rapid plasma reagin test.** Pityriasis rosea starts with a herald patch and then a "Christmas tree" rash and resolves on its own in 6–8 weeks. Secondary syphilis can look similar, so a sexually active patient should be tested, especially if palms or soles are involved.

**8. E — Topical metronidazole.** Rosacea causes flushing, redness, and papules and pustules without comedones, which separates it from acne. Topical metronidazole, azelaic acid, or ivermectin is first-line; oral doxycycline is used for more severe cases. Avoiding triggers helps.

**9. C — Chest X-ray.** Erythema nodosum is inflammation of fat under the skin linked to streptococcal infection, sarcoidosis, inflammatory bowel disease, drugs, and pregnancy. Fever, ankle pain, and erythema nodosum suggest Löfgren syndrome, so a chest X-ray looks for enlarged hilar lymph nodes.

**10. D — Surgical debridement.** Pyoderma gangrenosum worsens with injury (pathergy), so cutting it makes it bigger. It is associated with inflammatory bowel disease, arthritis, and blood cancers. Treatment is systemic steroids or other immunosuppressants and good wound care.

**11. C — Second-generation antihistamine.** Chronic hives (over 6 weeks) usually have no identifiable cause. A non-sedating antihistamine taken daily is first-line and can be increased up to four times the usual dose. Omalizumab is used if this fails. Extensive testing is rarely helpful.

**12. E — Patch testing.** Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed (type IV) reaction; nickel is the most common cause. Patch testing reproduces delayed reactions. Skin prick testing detects immediate (IgE) allergy.

**13. B — Topical ketoconazole.** Seborrheic dermatitis is linked to Malassezia yeast and is more common and severe in Parkinson disease and HIV. Antifungal shampoos or creams, with short courses of mild topical steroid, control it.

**14. C — HIV test.** Molluscum contagiosum is common and harmless in children. Many lesions on the face of an adult, especially large or widespread ones, suggest weakened immunity, most often from HIV.

**15. D — Gluten-free diet.** Dermatitis herpetiformis is the skin form of celiac disease, with IgA deposits in the tips of the dermal papillae. Dapsone controls itching quickly, and a strict gluten-free diet treats the cause and lowers lymphoma risk.

**16. D — Autoimmune thyroid disease.** Vitiligo is autoimmune destruction of pigment cells and often occurs with autoimmune thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anemia, and Addison disease. Checking TSH is reasonable.

**17. A — Squamous cell carcinoma.** A cancer arising in an old burn scar or chronic wound is called a Marjolin ulcer and is usually squamous cell carcinoma. It tends to be aggressive. Any non-healing ulcer in a scar should be biopsied.

**18. A — Oral propranolol.** Most infantile hemangiomas grow for months and then shrink. Treatment is needed when they threaten function: near the eye they can cause amblyopia. Propranolol is first-line for problem hemangiomas.

**19. D — Emergency surgery.** Necrotizing fasciitis spreads along fascia quickly. Pain out of proportion, rapid spread, dusky skin, and crepitus mean surgery is needed at once, with broad IV antibiotics including clindamycin. Imaging must not delay the operation.

**20. C — Incision and drainage.** A fluctuant abscess needs drainage; antibiotics alone cannot clear the pus. Antibiotics covering MRSA are added for large abscesses, fever, cellulitis around it, or weakened immunity.
