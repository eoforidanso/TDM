# Rheumatology — Practice Questions, Set 2

## Questions

## Answers and explanations

**1. C — Allopurinol with colchicine prophylaxis.** Urate-lowering therapy is started after two or more attacks a year, tophi, or urate kidney stones, aiming for uric acid below 6 mg/dL. Starting it can trigger attacks, so low-dose colchicine or an NSAID is given for the first 3–6 months. Testing for HLA-B*58:01 is advised in people of Southeast Asian or African descent before allopurinol.

**2. D — Anti-dsDNA and complement.** Anti-dsDNA levels rise and C3 and C4 fall when lupus, especially lupus nephritis, is active. ANA is used for screening, not monitoring. Anti-Smith is very specific but does not track activity.

**3. E — Steroids with mycophenolate.** Class III and IV lupus nephritis are the most serious types and need intensive treatment: glucocorticoids plus mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide. Hydroxychloroquine is continued in all lupus patients.

**4. B — Retinal eye examination.** Hydroxychloroquine can damage the retina. A baseline exam is done, then yearly screening after 5 years (sooner with risk factors such as kidney disease or high dose).

**5. B — B-cell lymphoma.** Sjögren syndrome carries a much higher risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, often of the parotid gland (MALT type). Persistent gland swelling, low complement, and cryoglobulins are warning signs.

**6. A — Congenital heart block.** Maternal anti-Ro antibodies cross the placenta and can damage the baby's heart conduction system. Fetal heart rate is monitored between about 16 and 26 weeks. The baby can also have a lupus-like rash.

**7. B — Screen for cancer.** Dermatomyositis is linked to cancers including ovarian, lung, stomach, colon, and lymphoma, especially in the first few years. Age-appropriate screening plus targeted imaging is done. Treatment is glucocorticoids with a steroid-sparing drug.

**8. C — Reactive arthritis.** It follows chlamydia or gut infections (Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, Yersinia) by 1–4 weeks, with asymmetric arthritis of large leg joints, eye inflammation, and urethritis. Joint fluid is sterile. NSAIDs are first-line; HLA-B27 is often positive.

**9. A — Pencil-in-cup deformity.** Psoriatic arthritis affects the distal joints, causes dactylitis and nail pitting, and erodes bone so the end of one bone looks like a pencil in a cup. Punched-out erosions with overhanging edges suggest gout.

**10. A — Exercise and duloxetine.** Fibromyalgia is managed with education, graded aerobic exercise, sleep hygiene, and cognitive behavioral therapy. Duloxetine, milnacipran, pregabalin, or low-dose amitriptyline may help. Opioids and steroids do not.

**11. A — IV ceftriaxone.** Disseminated gonococcal infection causes the triad of migrating joint pain, tenosynovitis, and skin pustules. Treatment is ceftriaxone, plus doxycycline for chlamydia unless it is excluded.

**12. D — Behçet disease.** It is a vasculitis common along the old Silk Road. It causes recurrent mouth and genital ulcers, uveitis, and pathergy (a pustule after skin prick). Colchicine and steroids are used, with stronger immunosuppression for eye or nervous system involvement.

**13. A — Takayasu arteritis.** It is inflammation of the aorta and its main branches, mostly in young Asian women. It causes weak pulses, arm claudication, and different blood pressures between arms ("pulseless disease"). Steroids are first-line.

**14. C — IV immunoglobulin and aspirin.** Kawasaki disease is fever for 5 days or more with four of five signs: conjunctivitis, mouth changes, rash, swollen hands and feet, and a neck lymph node. IVIG given within 10 days reduces coronary aneurysms. An echocardiogram is done at diagnosis.

**15. B — Polyarteritis nodosa.** It affects medium-sized arteries in the kidney, gut, nerves, and skin, with aneurysms on angiography. It is associated with hepatitis B, is ANCA-negative, and spares the lungs.

**16. D — Intussusception.** IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein purpura) causes palpable purpura with normal platelets, abdominal pain, arthritis, and sometimes kidney disease. Swelling of the bowel wall can lead to intussusception. Urinalysis and blood pressure are followed for months.

**17. A — Folic acid.** Methotrexate blocks folate metabolism. Folic acid reduces mouth ulcers, nausea, liver enzyme rises, and low blood counts without reducing benefit. Blood count, liver tests, and creatinine are checked regularly.

**18. A — Latent tuberculosis screening.** TNF is needed to keep tuberculosis walled off in granulomas, so TNF inhibitors can reactivate latent TB. A TB skin test or interferon-gamma release assay is done first, along with hepatitis B testing.

**19. E — Osteoarthritis.** Bony enlargement of the distal finger joints (Heberden nodes) and middle joints (Bouchard nodes) with brief stiffness is typical of osteoarthritis. Rheumatoid arthritis spares the distal joints and causes soft swelling and long morning stiffness.

**20. D — Pulmonary arterial hypertension.** Limited systemic sclerosis (CREST: calcinosis, Raynaud, esophageal problems, sclerodactyly, telangiectasia) is linked to anti-centromere antibodies and to pulmonary hypertension. Yearly echocardiograms and lung function tests are recommended. Kidney crisis is more typical of the diffuse form.
