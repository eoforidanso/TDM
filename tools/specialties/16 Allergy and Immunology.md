# Allergy and Immunology — Practice Questions, Set 2

## Answers and explanations

**1. A — Evaluate, then introduce peanut early.** Infants with severe eczema or egg allergy are at high risk of peanut allergy. Introducing peanut at around 4–6 months, after evaluation for existing allergy, greatly reduces the chance of developing it. Delaying peanut increases risk.

**2. E — Penicillin skin testing.** Most people labeled penicillin-allergic are not allergic now; IgE allergy fades over years. With a history of immediate hives and penicillin needed, skin testing followed by an oral challenge, or desensitization if positive, is the route. Premedication does not prevent anaphylaxis.

**3. D — Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome.** It is an X-linked disorder with eczema, low platelets that are small, and recurrent infections, caused by WAS gene mutations. It carries a raised risk of autoimmune disease and lymphoma; stem cell transplant is curative.

**4. E — Severe combined immunodeficiency.** Absent T cells (and functionally absent B cells) cause severe infections in the first months, chronic diarrhea, thrush, and Pneumocystis. No thymic shadow is seen. Newborn screening (TREC assay) detects it. Live vaccines are avoided and stem cell transplant is needed.

**5. B — CD40 ligand.** Without CD40 ligand on helper T cells, B cells cannot switch from making IgM to other classes. Hyper-IgM syndrome causes recurrent bacterial infections and opportunistic ones such as Pneumocystis and Cryptosporidium.

**6. E — Hyper-IgE syndrome.** Usually from STAT3 mutation (Job syndrome): eczema, "cold" staphylococcal abscesses without warmth or redness, recurrent pneumonia with cavities, retained baby teeth, coarse facial features, and very high IgE.

**7. B — CH50.** Recurrent Neisseria infections suggest deficiency of the terminal complement components (C5–C9) that form the membrane attack complex. CH50 is a screening test; it is low when any classical pathway component is missing.

**8. B — Chediak–Higashi syndrome.** A defect in lysosomal trafficking (LYST gene) causes giant granules in white cells, partial albinism, nerve damage, bleeding, and recurrent infections.

**9. C — Leukocyte adhesion deficiency.** White cells lack CD18 integrins and cannot leave the bloodstream, so counts are very high but no pus forms at infection sites. Delayed separation of the umbilical cord is a classic early sign.

**10. B — Prednisone with itraconazole.** Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis is a hypersensitivity reaction to Aspergillus in people with asthma or cystic fibrosis. Steroids reduce inflammation and itraconazole reduces the fungal load. Untreated, it causes central bronchiectasis.

**11. A — DRESS.** Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms starts 2–8 weeks after a drug such as an anticonvulsant, allopurinol, or sulfonamide. It causes fever, rash, facial swelling, eosinophilia, and organ damage, often to the liver. The drug is stopped and systemic steroids are often needed.

**12. C — Oral allergy syndrome.** Antibodies to birch pollen cross-react with similar proteins in raw fruits such as apples. Cooking destroys these proteins. Symptoms are usually limited to the mouth.

**13. B — Banana, avocado, and kiwi.** Latex proteins cross-react with proteins in banana, avocado, kiwi, and chestnut (the latex-fruit syndrome). Latex-allergic patients need latex-free gloves and equipment.

**14. C — Premedicate.** For a past mild to moderate reaction to contrast, guidelines recommend premedication with a corticosteroid (such as prednisone 13, 7, and 1 hour before) and an antihistamine, or choosing a different contrast agent. Seafood allergy does not predict contrast reactions.

**15. B — Any age-appropriate influenza vaccine.** Current influenza vaccines contain very little egg protein. People with egg allergy of any severity can receive them in the usual setting without extra precautions.

**16. D — Pneumococcal, meningococcal, and Hib.** The spleen clears encapsulated bacteria. People without one are at risk of overwhelming infection with Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, and Haemophilus influenzae. Annual influenza vaccine is also advised.

**17. C — Type IV, delayed.** The tuberculin reaction is driven by memory T cells that recruit macrophages over 48–72 hours. Contact dermatitis is also type IV.

**18. A — Anti-D immunoglobulin.** Giving anti-D at 28 weeks, and again within 72 hours after delivery of an Rh-positive baby, prevents the mother from forming anti-D antibodies that would attack red cells in future pregnancies.

**19. E — Venom immunotherapy.** For adults with a systemic reaction to an insect sting, venom immunotherapy reduces the risk of future anaphylaxis from about 60% to under 5%. An epinephrine autoinjector is also prescribed.

**20. B — Lymphoma.** Acquired C1 inhibitor deficiency appears later in life without family history, usually with B-cell lymphoproliferative disease or autoantibodies to C1 inhibitor. Low C1q separates it from hereditary angioedema, in which C1q is normal.
