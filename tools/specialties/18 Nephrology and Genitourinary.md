## Answers and explanations

**1. B — Saline with potassium.** Vomiting causes metabolic alkalosis from loss of stomach acid and volume. Low urine chloride (under 20) means the alkalosis is saline-responsive: volume loss makes the kidney hold onto bicarbonate. Saline with potassium chloride corrects it.

**2. D — Negative.** In diarrhea, the kidneys respond correctly to acidosis by excreting more ammonium (with chloride), so urine chloride exceeds sodium plus potassium and the urine anion gap is negative. In distal RTA, the kidney cannot excrete acid, and the gap is positive.

**3. D — Distal (type 1) RTA.** The distal tubule cannot secrete acid, so urine pH stays above 5.5 despite acidosis. Low potassium and calcium phosphate stones are typical. Causes include Sjögren syndrome, lupus, and amphotericin B. Treatment is alkali.

**4. D — Type 4 RTA.** Low renin and aldosterone, common in diabetic kidney disease, cause high potassium and mild acidosis. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, NSAIDs, and potassium-sparing diuretics worsen it. Treatment includes a low-potassium diet, loop diuretics, and sometimes fludrocortisone.

**5. B — IV isotonic fluid.** Volume expansion with saline or bicarbonate before and after contrast is the main preventive measure in people with reduced kidney function. N-acetylcysteine has not shown clear benefit. The smallest effective contrast dose is used.

**6. C — Stop the drug.** Acute interstitial nephritis is an allergic reaction in the kidney, most often to proton pump inhibitors, antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins), or NSAIDs. Fever, rash, and white cell casts are typical. Stopping the drug is the key step; steroids may help if kidney function does not recover.

**7. A — Bilateral renal artery stenosis.** When both renal arteries are narrowed, the kidneys depend on angiotensin II to maintain filtration, so ACE inhibitors cause creatinine to rise sharply. Sudden ("flash") pulmonary edema and an abdominal bruit are clues.

**8. C — Angioplasty.** Fibromuscular dysplasia causes renovascular hypertension in young women. Unlike atherosclerotic stenosis, it responds well to balloon angioplasty, which often cures the hypertension.

**9. B — Berry aneurysm.** Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease is linked to intracranial aneurysms, especially with a family history of brain hemorrhage. Other associations include liver cysts and mitral valve prolapse. Screening MRA is advised for those with a family history.

**10. A — Collapsing FSGS.** HIV-associated nephropathy is a collapsing form of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, most common in people of African descent with APOL1 risk variants. It causes heavy proteinuria and rapid loss of kidney function. Antiretroviral therapy and ACE inhibitors are the treatment.

**11. A — IgA nephropathy.** Visible hematuria within 1–2 days of an upper respiratory infection, often recurring, with normal complement, is typical. It is the most common glomerulonephritis worldwide. Post-streptococcal disease appears weeks later with low C3.

**12. D — Plasma exchange, steroids, and cyclophosphamide.** Anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture syndrome) attacks type IV collagen in the lungs and kidneys. Linear IgG on biopsy is diagnostic. Plasma exchange removes the antibody while immunosuppression stops its production.

**13. E — Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules.** Diabetic nephropathy causes thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, mesangial expansion, and nodular glomerulosclerosis. It is the leading cause of end-stage kidney disease. Spike-and-dome deposits suggest membranous nephropathy.

**14. D — Iron first.** Erythropoietin does not work well without adequate iron. In chronic kidney disease, iron is given when ferritin is under about 500 ng/mL and transferrin saturation is under 30%; IV iron is often used. Erythropoietin is started if hemoglobin stays below 10 g/dL.

**15. E — Phosphate binder with meals.** High phosphate drives secondary hyperparathyroidism and vascular calcification. Diet restriction and a binder taken with meals, such as sevelamer, lower it. Calcium-based binders are limited when calcium is already high-normal. Calcitriol raises phosphate.

**16. E — Uremic pericarditis.** Urgent dialysis indications are refractory acidosis, refractory high potassium, ingestion of a dialyzable poison, fluid overload not responding to diuretics, and uremic complications such as pericarditis, encephalopathy, or bleeding. A creatinine value alone is not an indication.

**17. D — D5W with slow correction.** In a stable patient with high sodium from water loss, free water is replaced slowly to avoid brain swelling. Hypotensive patients get isotonic saline first.

**18. D — Magnesium.** Low magnesium increases potassium loss in the kidney, and potassium cannot be corrected until magnesium is replaced. Low magnesium is common with alcohol use, diarrhea, and diuretics.

**19. D — Water drawn out of cells.** High glucose raises serum osmolality and pulls water from cells, diluting sodium. Sodium falls by about 1.6–2.4 mEq/L for every 100 mg/dL rise in glucose. Treating the glucose corrects the sodium.

**20. D — Renal vein thrombosis.** Nephrotic syndrome causes a clotting tendency from urinary loss of anticoagulant proteins such as antithrombin III. Membranous nephropathy carries the highest risk of renal vein thrombosis. Anticoagulation is the treatment.
