## Answers and explanations

**1. C — Hemoglobin electrophoresis.** Very small red cells with mild anemia, a high red cell count, and normal iron stores suggest thalassemia trait (Mentzer index, MCV divided by red cell count, under 13). Electrophoresis shows raised HbA2 in beta-thalassemia trait; alpha-thalassemia trait may need genetic testing.

**2. E — Low iron, low TIBC, normal or high ferritin.** In anemia of chronic disease, hepcidin traps iron in storage. Serum iron is low, but transferrin (TIBC) is low and ferritin is normal or high. Iron deficiency has high TIBC and low ferritin.

**3. C — G6PD deficiency.** Oxidizing drugs (sulfonamides, primaquine, dapsone), fava beans, or infection trigger hemolysis. Heinz bodies are denatured hemoglobin, and macrophages remove them, leaving bite cells. The level can be falsely normal during an attack, so testing is repeated later.

**4. A — EMA binding test.** Hereditary spherocytosis comes from defects in red cell membrane proteins such as spectrin and ankyrin. It causes spherocytes, high MCHC, a negative antiglobulin test, splenomegaly, and gallstones. Flow cytometry for eosin-5-maleimide binding is the preferred test.

**5. E — Glucocorticoids.** Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (IgG antibodies, often with lupus, lymphoma, or drugs) is treated first with steroids. Rituximab or splenectomy is used if it does not respond.

**6. A — Treat the cause.** Disseminated intravascular coagulation uses up clotting factors and platelets. Treating the underlying cause, here sepsis, is the key step. Platelets, plasma, or cryoprecipitate are given for bleeding or before procedures.

**7. D — Desmopressin.** Von Willebrand disease is the most common inherited bleeding disorder and causes mucosal bleeding. Desmopressin releases von Willebrand factor stored in blood vessel walls and works in most cases of type 1. Tranexamic acid helps for dental work.

**8. A — Factor VIII concentrate.** Hemophilia A is X-linked, so boys are affected through their mothers' families. Bleeding into joints is typical. PTT is prolonged and corrects with mixing (a missing factor, not an inhibitor). Hemophilia B (factor IX) looks the same and is distinguished by factor assays.

**9. D — Hold warfarin and give oral vitamin K.** For an INR above 10 without bleeding, warfarin is held and oral vitamin K is given; between 4.5 and 10 without bleeding, holding warfarin is usually enough. IV vitamin K and factor concentrates are for serious bleeding.

**10. A — Prothrombin complex and IV vitamin K.** Life-threatening bleeding on warfarin needs immediate reversal. Four-factor PCC replaces factors II, VII, IX, and X within minutes, and IV vitamin K keeps the reversal going. Protamine reverses heparin; idarucizumab reverses dabigatran.

**11. D — Phlebotomy and aspirin.** Polycythemia vera is a JAK2-driven bone marrow disorder. Phlebotomy to a hematocrit below 45% and low-dose aspirin reduce clotting. Hydroxyurea is added for patients at high risk, such as those over 60 or with prior clots.

**12. D — Stop the transfusion.** Fever, back pain, red urine, and low blood pressure early in a transfusion suggest acute hemolysis from ABO incompatibility. Stop at once, keep the IV open with saline, recheck patient and blood unit identity, and send samples to the blood bank.

**13. A — Glycopyrrolate and reassurance.** "Death rattle" is caused by secretions the dying patient can no longer clear and is usually not distressing to the patient. Positioning, anticholinergic drugs, and explaining it to the family help. Deep suctioning is uncomfortable and rarely helps.

**14. C — Haloperidol.** Haloperidol is first-line for delirium at the end of life. Benzodiazepines alone can worsen delirium but may be added for severe agitation. Anticholinergic drugs such as diphenhydramine also worsen confusion.

**15. D — 20 mg.** Oral morphine is about one-third as potent as IV morphine because of first-pass metabolism in the liver, so 60 mg orally is about 20 mg IV. When switching between different opioids, the dose is reduced further for incomplete cross-tolerance.

**16. A — Switch to fentanyl.** Morphine's active metabolites build up in kidney failure and cause myoclonus, confusion, and sedation. Fentanyl and methadone are safer in kidney failure. Codeine and tramadol also have metabolites that accumulate.

**17. C — Radiation therapy.** External beam radiation, often as a single dose, relieves pain from a bone metastasis in most patients within weeks. Bisphosphonates or denosumab reduce future skeletal events.

**18. C — Octreotide.** Octreotide reduces gut secretions, decreasing vomiting and distension in malignant bowel obstruction. Antiemetics such as haloperidol, steroids, and a venting gastrostomy tube may also help. Prokinetics like metoclopramide are avoided in complete obstruction.

**19. A — Normal grief.** Sadness, poor sleep, and even briefly hearing or sensing the dead person are common in normal grief. She is still functioning and has no feelings of worthlessness or suicidal thoughts. Support and follow-up are appropriate; prolonged grief disorder is considered after a year.

**20. D — Palliative care alongside treatment.** Palliative care treats symptoms and supports patients at any stage, together with cancer treatment, and improves quality of life. Hospice is a specific form of palliative care for the last months of life, when the patient chooses to stop disease-directed treatment.
