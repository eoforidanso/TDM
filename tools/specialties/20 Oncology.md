# Oncology — Practice Questions, Set 2

USMLE Step 2 CK level. Twenty original questions. Answers and explanations are at the bottom.

## Answers and explanations

**1. B — Pap smear.** For ages 21–29, cervical cytology every 3 years is recommended; HPV testing is not used alone in this age group because HPV infection is common and usually clears. From 30–65, options include HPV testing every 5 years or cytology every 3 years.

**2. C — Mammography every 2 years.** Current US Preventive Services Task Force guidance recommends screening mammography every 2 years for average-risk women aged 40–74. MRI is for women at high risk, such as BRCA carriers.

**3. B — Genetic counseling.** Early-onset breast cancer and ovarian cancer in close relatives suggest a hereditary syndrome such as BRCA1 or BRCA2. Counseling decides whether genetic testing is appropriate and explains what positive results would mean for screening and prevention.

**4. D — Echocardiogram.** Trastuzumab can weaken the heart muscle, usually reversibly. Ejection fraction is measured before starting and every 3 months during treatment. The risk is higher with anthracyclines.

**5. B — Endometrial cancer.** Tamoxifen blocks estrogen in the breast but stimulates the endometrium, raising the risk of endometrial cancer, as well as blood clots and stroke. It protects bone. Abnormal vaginal bleeding on tamoxifen needs evaluation.

**6. B — Bone density scan.** Aromatase inhibitors block estrogen production after menopause and cause bone loss and fractures. A baseline DEXA scan, calcium, vitamin D, and bisphosphonates if needed are standard. Joint pain is a common side effect.

**7. A — CT of the chest with contrast.** Superior vena cava syndrome is most often caused by lung cancer or lymphoma. In a stable patient, imaging and a tissue diagnosis come before treatment, because the right treatment depends on the type of cancer. Stenting is used for severe symptoms such as airway compromise.

**8. C — Small cell carcinoma.** Lambert–Eaton syndrome results from antibodies against presynaptic calcium channels. It causes proximal weakness that improves with repeated use and autonomic symptoms such as dry mouth. It is most often linked to small cell lung cancer, which is central and associated with smoking.

**9. C — Pancoast tumor.** A tumor at the top of the lung can invade the brachial plexus (shoulder and arm pain) and the sympathetic chain, causing Horner syndrome: ptosis, a small pupil, and loss of sweating. It is usually a non-small cell cancer.

**10. A — No further follow-up.** Popcorn calcification is characteristic of a hamartoma, a benign tumor. Benign calcification patterns (popcorn, central, laminated, or diffuse) do not need follow-up. Non-calcified nodules are managed by size and risk.

**11. E — Carcinoembryonic antigen.** CEA is measured before surgery and then regularly afterwards to detect recurrence, along with CT scans and colonoscopy. It is not used for screening because it can be raised by smoking and inflammation.

**12. D — Ultrasound every 6 months.** People with cirrhosis from any cause, and some with hepatitis B without cirrhosis, should have liver ultrasound every 6 months, with or without alpha-fetoprotein, to find hepatocellular carcinoma early.

**13. A — Pancreas-protocol CT.** Painless jaundice with a palpable, nontender gallbladder (Courvoisier sign) suggests obstruction of the bile duct by a tumor at the head of the pancreas. A dedicated CT diagnoses and stages it. CA 19-9 supports the diagnosis and is used for monitoring.

**14. A — Gynecologic oncologist.** A pelvic mass with ascites and high CA-125 in a postmenopausal woman strongly suggests ovarian cancer. Surgery for staging and maximal removal by a gynecologic oncologist improves survival. Needle biopsy through the skin risks spreading tumor cells.

**15. C — Endometrial biopsy.** Any bleeding after menopause needs evaluation for endometrial cancer. Endometrial biopsy or transvaginal ultrasound (an endometrial thickness of 4 mm or less makes cancer unlikely) is the first step. Obesity, unopposed estrogen, and tamoxifen are risk factors.

**16. A — Urine 5-HIAA.** Carcinoid syndrome occurs when a neuroendocrine tumor, usually with liver metastases, releases serotonin into the general circulation. It causes flushing, diarrhea, wheezing, and right-sided heart valve disease. 5-HIAA is a serotonin breakdown product.

**17. B — Lung cancer.** Lung cancer is the most common source of brain metastases, followed by breast cancer, melanoma, kidney cancer, and colon cancer. Metastases tend to lodge at the gray–white junction where blood vessels narrow, and are often multiple.

**18. D — Yearly monitoring.** This is monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance: M-protein under 3 g/dL, under 10% plasma cells, and no signs of end-organ damage (high calcium, kidney injury, anemia, bone lesions). It progresses to myeloma or a related disease at about 1% a year, so it is monitored, not treated.

**19. A — All-trans retinoic acid.** Acute promyelocytic leukemia carries the t(15;17) translocation (PML-RARA). It often presents with DIC and is an emergency. ATRA, usually with arsenic trioxide, makes the cancer cells mature and has high cure rates. Treatment starts on suspicion.

**20. D — Observation.** Chronic lymphocytic leukemia is common in older adults and often found incidentally. Early-stage disease without symptoms, low blood counts, or large nodes or spleen is monitored; early treatment does not improve survival.
